PROJECT:
CLEANING_ROVER

✓ OPERATIONAL EMBEDDED SYSTEMS ROBOTICS IoT

01. Objective

The goal of this project was to build an autonomous cleaning rover that could regularly clean the room it's placed in, completely unattended. The rover needed to scan its surroundings, avoid obstacles, and drive a brush mechanism — all while being controllable remotely over WiFi.

03. The Challenges

The first challenge was selecting the right motors and driver. The 130 DC motor was initially chosen for its small size and high RPM, but its native torque was far too low to move the chassis under load.

The second challenge was the power system — selecting the correct battery configuration, regulators, and protection circuitry for reliable long-term operation.

The third challenge was a logic level mismatch between the HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors and the ESP32. The ESP32 operates on 3.3V logic, but the sensors' Echo pin outputs a 5V signal. Connecting this directly would have damaged the microcontroller's GPIO registers.

04. The Solutions

Motor & Driver: The initial approach used a 3D-printed 5:1 gear reduction (12T to 60T) with an L298N driver. This solved the torque issue in theory, but caused a significant voltage drop the moment the wheels touched the ground and the motors drew current — threatening the circuit. The fix was to switch to TT gear motors with built-in reduction and replace the L298N with a DRV8833. The DRV8833 is far more efficient, generates much less heat, and handles the current draw without voltage collapse.

Power System: A 3S2P 18650 Li-ion pack provides 11.1V with a 3S BMS board for protection. Two separate rails step the voltage down — an EY9 regulator provides stable 5V for the ESP32 and sensors, while an XL4015 buck converter is set to 6V for the DRV8833 motor drivers.

// Power Architecture SOURCE 3S2P 18650 Li-ion → 11.1V [BMS Protected] RAIL A EY9 Regulator → 5V [ESP32 + Sensors] RAIL B XL4015 Buck Conv → 6V [DRV8833 Drive Motors] MONITOR ACS712 + V-Divider → GPIO34/35 [Current + Voltage ADC]

Logic Level Fix: A voltage divider (two resistors per sensor) steps the 5V Echo signal down to a safe 3.3V before it reaches the ESP32 GPIO. Four of these are placed for the four sensors scanning the robot's surroundings.

Brush Motors: The original 130 DC motors were repurposed to drive the cleaning brushes, managed by a second DRV8833 integrated into the schematic.

WiFi Interface: A full web dashboard was built and served directly from the ESP32, accessible from any device on the same network. It includes:

Radar Obstacle Display Manual Drive Controls Auto Avoidance Mode Motor Speed Sliders Brush Speed Control Battery Voltage Monitor

07. Lessons Learned

Motor Selection

Never select a motor based on RPM alone. Torque under load is the critical spec — the 130 DC motor looked ideal on paper but failed the moment the chassis weight was applied. TT gear motors with built-in reduction were the right call from the start.

Driver Efficiency Matters

The L298N is a legacy driver with significant heat dissipation and voltage drop. The DRV8833 is a far better fit for battery-powered mobile robotics — lower dropout, less heat, smaller footprint.

Logic Level Mismatches Are Silent Killers

The 5V Echo signal issue could have permanently damaged the ESP32. Always check logic levels before connecting sensors — a simple two-resistor voltage divider is a cheap insurance policy.

Power Rails Should Be Isolated

Separating the logic rail (5V) from the motor rail (6V) was essential. Motors draw large, noisy currents that can cause voltage dips and glitch the microcontroller if they share a rail.
