01. Objective

Assessment Task 2 (50% of course mark) for OENG1250 — Introduction to Engineering Design at RMIT University. The brief placed students in the context of Australia's $150M Moon to Mars initiative: design a rover capable of supporting a NASA In-Situ Resource Utilisation (ISRU) facility on the lunar surface.

Group 13 (6 members) divided the rover into subsystems — chassis/body, wheels, suspension, payload compartment, and power system. My assigned component was the power system: the solar panel and 18650 lithium-ion battery pack.

I was responsible for all design stages of the power system — from initial hand-drawn concept sketches through SolidWorks preliminary models, detailed part drawings, and the final integrated sub-assembly — producing 9 technical drawings as individual deliverables.

The 354-hour operational requirement was particularly demanding — the lunar day lasts approximately 29.5 Earth days, so the rover needed to sustain operations through extended periods of reduced solar input. This directly shaped the battery capacity calculation.

04. Power System Design

The power system consists of two integrated components: a 12V lithium-ion battery pack (32× 18650 cells) and a 20W solar panel mounted on the rover's top surface. The design went through two formal iteration stages — preliminary and detailed — with geometry and calculations refined at each stage.

Battery Selection: 18650-format lithium-ion cells were chosen for their established spaceflight heritage (used in Tesla packs, power tools, and research rover prototypes), high energy density, cylindrical form factor enabling dense packing, and compatibility with the 12V system voltage when arranged in series/parallel configuration.

Configuration: 32 cells arranged in a 4S8P configuration (4 cells in series × 8 parallel strings) — producing 12V nominal with 2400 Wh total usable capacity at a safety factor of 1.2 applied to operation time calculations.

05. Design Iteration

The assessment required two formal design stages. Each stage produced SolidWorks part files, drawing files (.SLDDRW), and PDF technical drawings exported to A3 format.

Week 3–4 · Preliminary Design
Initial Geometry + Layout Concept
Hand-drawn concept sketches established the stacked layout — solar panel sits on top of a rectangular battery storage compartment. SolidWorks parts created for the single 18650 cell (B00-1), the preliminary battery pack housing (PP-001), and the preliminary solar panel (SP-001). Key decision: cells arranged horizontally in 4 rows with thermal spacing gaps between each cell to allow passive heat dissipation in the vacuum environment.
Week 5–6 · Detailed Design
Refined Models + Full Pack Geometry
Detailed 18650 cell model (B00-2) introduced asymmetric terminal fillet geometry — matching the actual physical cell's positive and negative terminal profiles, with Ø0.5mm pin detail and 2mm cap thickness. Battery pack detailed design (PP-002) added hanging mounting brackets so the pack suspends from the rover body frame rather than resting on the floor — accommodating wiring harnesses underneath. Solar panel (SP-002) refined with corner bracket mounting tabs (Ø8mm bolt pattern) and 5mm frame rail detail.
Week 7 · Sub-Assembly
Integrated Power System Assembly (AS-002)
The solar panel (SP-001) and battery pack assembly (AS-001) were integrated with the rover body preliminary part into a complete power sub-assembly (AS-002). The drawing produced a full A3 sheet with two isometric views and orthographic projections with key dimensions. This sub-assembly was then referenced in the full rover assembly produced by the team.

08. Lessons Learned

Thermal Spacing Matters Even in Vacuum

The initial preliminary model packed cells with zero gap — maximising energy density. After reviewing thermal management requirements for the lunar environment, gaps were added between cell rows in the detailed design to allow passive conductive heat paths to the housing walls. In a vacuum, convection is impossible; the cells need a direct conductive path to any radiating surface. This iteration wasn't intuitive at first but became obvious once the heat transfer physics were considered.

Mounting Method Determines Assembly Order

The preliminary battery pack sat on the rover floor — straightforward in CAD. When the detailed design added hanging brackets (the pack suspends from the frame top), the assembly sequence changed completely. The pack had to be installed before the side panels were closed. This showed that manufacturing and assembly order must be considered at the detail design stage, not at integration.

SolidWorks Mate Conflicts Are Caught Early or Expensively

Integrating the sub-assembly into the team's rover assembly triggered a number of mate conflicts — coordinate system origins that didn't align between my models and teammates' body components. The fix was straightforward (re-defining the reference geometry), but it took time at integration that could have been avoided if the team had agreed on a shared origin convention at Week 3.

Weight Budget Is a Team-Level Constraint

The ≤20 kg rover mass limit is shared across all subsystems. My power system came in at ~3.6 kg (battery 1440g + solar panel 2157g + housing), which consumed roughly 18% of the total mass budget. Without a team-level weight spreadsheet tracked from the start, it's easy for individual components to creep in mass without anyone noticing until the assembly is too heavy.
