Lab 1 — Demand Response in a Residential Distribution Network

Setup

The network model consisted of 36 residential loads connected across a 0.433 kV LV distribution feeder supplied from an external grid. A 13% load shift was applied (10% base + 3 from student ID last digit), redistributing that fraction of peak demand to off-peak periods. Simulations were run for four seasons (summer, autumn, winter, spring) under two pricing regimes: Victorian Time-of-Use (ToU) tariffs and volatile AEMO half-hourly spot prices.

Task 3 — ToU vs AEMO Pricing: Which Incentivises DR More?

ToU pricing delivered consistently larger DR savings across all seasons. Under AEMO pricing, summer costs were already negative at baseline — meaning demand response had almost no financial benefit (and could even worsen costs by shifting load into higher-price afternoon slots).

ToU wins
Time-of-Use tariffs provide stronger and more predictable demand response incentives than volatile AEMO spot pricing

The key reason: AEMO spot prices are highly volatile and unpredictable at the retail scale. Consumers cannot reliably time load shifts to match spot price movements. ToU rates have fixed peak/off-peak windows — a household can confidently shift the dishwasher to 11pm and always capture the full benefit. The conclusion was that ToU tariffs are the more effective DR signal for residential customers, while AEMO-linked pricing may suit large commercial/industrial consumers with dedicated energy management systems.

Lab 3 — Community Microgrid: PV + BESS + Diesel + Grid

Network Overview

The same 36-load LV network was equipped with a community-scale microgrid: 12 solar PV generators, one Battery Energy Storage System (BESS), one 100 kW diesel generator, and an external grid connection point. Quasi-dynamic load flow simulations were run over a full 24-hour period using real sunny-day and cloudy-day PV profiles. Four study cases were defined:

Study Case 01
Solar PV + BESS (Islanded)
PV charges BESS by day; BESS supplies load overnight. No external grid. Tested under sunny and cloudy conditions.
Study Case 02
Diesel + BESS (Islanded)
Diesel generator supplements BESS under cloudy conditions when PV is insufficient. No grid connection.
Study Case 03
PV + BESS + Grid (Connected)
External grid replaces diesel as backup. Grid imports overnight when BESS is depleted. Cloudy-day conditions.
Study Case 04
Grid + BESS + Capacitor Bank
Capacitor bank (0.055 MVAr) added at Bus 43 to resolve voltage violations introduced by Case 03 grid imports.

Task 1 — PV + BESS on a Sunny Day (Case 01)

On a sunny day, solar PV supplied all loads and recharged the BESS between 08:00–16:00. The BESS discharged overnight (18:00–08:00). The net 24-hour BESS balance was −6 kWh — essentially zero — confirming that PV fully sustained the islanded microgrid without any backup source. Bus 43 voltage remained within ±6% throughout, rising to 1.02 p.u. at 10:00–13:00 due to reverse power flow from PV generation.

Task 2 — PV + BESS on a Cloudy Day (Case 01)

Under cloudy conditions, PV generation was reduced to 08:00–14:00 only. The BESS could not sustain the network — the net balance turned positive at +886 kWh, indicating energy shortfall. Operating cost rose to $272.57 (~30% increase). Notably, Bus 43 voltage followed nearly the same profile as the sunny day, since the BESS acts as a voltage-generating source in islanded mode regardless of PV availability.

Task 3 — Diesel + BESS under Cloudy Conditions (Case 02)

A 100 kW diesel generator was added to cover the PV shortfall under cloudy conditions. The diesel ran overnight (00:00–02:30) and during the evening peak (16:00–22:00), supplying 950 kWh total. BESS handled transition periods, discharging 616 kWh at $0.20/kWh. Total operating cost jumped to $1,073.25 — a 420% increase over sunny-day BESS-only operation — because diesel costs $1.00/kWh (5× the BESS rate). Bus 43 voltage dipped to a minimum of 0.94 p.u. during the diesel-to-solar transition at 15:30, touching the lower limit but causing no violation.

Task 4 — Grid-Connected PV + BESS (Case 03)

Diesel was replaced with an external grid connection. The grid supplied ~98 kW overnight when BESS was depleted and exported small amounts (~2.8 kW) during peak PV periods. Total operating cost dropped to $450.63 — a 58% reduction vs diesel — because grid electricity at $0.35/kWh is far cheaper than diesel at $1.00/kWh.

However, grid connection introduced voltage violations at Bus 43. High reactive power demand from large grid imports along the LV feeder caused voltage to drop to 0.92 p.u. overnight and to a worst-case 0.91 p.u. at 16:00–16:30 (peak evening load + maximum grid import). Voltage stayed below the 0.94 p.u. lower limit for approximately 10 hours of the 24-hour period.

Task 5 — Capacitor Bank for Voltage Correction (Case 04)

A 0.055 MVAr capacitor bank was added at Bus 43 to supply reactive power locally and counteract the voltage drop from grid imports. The capacitor raised voltage by 0.03 p.u. across the entire 24-hour period, bringing the minimum from 0.91 p.u. to 0.94 p.u. — exactly on the lower limit with no violations. Operating cost increased by only $0.49 (negligible), confirming that reactive power compensation is highly cost-effective for voltage management in LV networks with large grid imports.

03. Key Results Summary

✅ Lab 1: 13% demand response under ToU pricing saves up to $20.38/day for the external grid in winter; individual households save $0.30–$0.50/day. ToU consistently outperforms AEMO pricing as a DR incentive.

✅ Lab 1: Summer AEMO baseline cost was negative (−$33.55/day) — renewable oversupply can eliminate retail grid costs entirely in solar-rich periods.

✅ Lab 3 (Case 01): Solar PV + BESS was fully self-sustaining on a sunny day (net balance −6 kWh). Cloudy day created 886 kWh shortfall — backup source required.

✅ Lab 3 (Case 02→03): Replacing diesel with grid connection cut operating cost by $622.62/day (58%) — $1.00/kWh diesel vs $0.35/kWh grid is the dominant economic factor.

✅ Lab 3 (Case 03→04): A 0.055 MVAr capacitor bank at Bus 43 eliminated all voltage violations at virtually no cost (+$0.49/day), raising minimum voltage from 0.91 to 0.94 p.u.

✅ Grid connection is the most cost-effective backup option but requires reactive power support at the weakest network bus. Demand response (shifting loads from evening peak) would directly reduce Bus 43 voltage stress.

04. Lessons Learned

ToU Tariffs Are a Better DR Signal Than Spot Prices

AEMO half-hourly prices are too volatile for residential customers to track — savings were smaller and inconsistent. Fixed ToU windows (peak/shoulder/off-peak) allow households to reliably shift load and always capture the full benefit. For large commercial consumers with automated energy management, spot-linked pricing can work, but residential DR needs simple, predictable pricing.

Diesel Costs 5× More Than Grid — Avoid It Wherever Possible

The Case 02 to Case 03 comparison was stark: $1.00/kWh diesel vs $0.35/kWh grid electricity drove a 58% cost reduction just by connecting to the grid. Islanded diesel backup is only justified where grid connection is physically or commercially unavailable.

Grid Connection Trades Cost for Voltage Risk

Connecting to the grid introduced voltage violations at Bus 43 that didn't exist in islanded mode. Long LV feeders with high impedance drop voltage badly under large grid imports. Reactive power compensation (a capacitor bank) is cheap and effective, but its location matters — placing it at the weak bus rather than the feeder head made the difference.

PV Self-Sufficiency Has a Clear Irradiance Threshold

The same PV + BESS system was fully self-sustaining on a sunny day (net −6 kWh) but left an 886 kWh daily shortfall under cloudy conditions. This highlights the need for grid connection or storage oversizing in climates with significant cloud cover — no islanded PV microgrid can be reliably self-sufficient year-round without backup generation.
